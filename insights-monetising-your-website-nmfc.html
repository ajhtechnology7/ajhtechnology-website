How a club website can support its income: what building Needham Market FC's site taught me

Most small organisations treat a website as a cost — a digital noticeboard you pay for and hope about. It doesn't have to be. This is my opinion on how a website can help bring money in, from building Needham Market FC's website as a volunteer for my own club. There are no revenue figures here — just what I've learned about where a site can help.

A website earns its keep when it makes it easier for people to give you money, or to ask to. Not by looking nicer on its own, and not by "raising awareness". By being the place a supporter, a sponsor or a customer can act when nobody is there to answer the phone.

The background

Needham Market FC is a century-old non-league club at Bloomfields, and it's my club. I built the site on my own, as a volunteer, in return for sponsorship — it wasn't a paid job — and I still add the matchday programmes and updates. Like most clubs, a lot of things used to happen over the phone, at the gate or in the clubhouse. That works, but only when someone is there.

Four ways a club website can support income

1. Booking requests for the things you hire out. This is the part I built in-house. On the Needham site you pick a facility — the 3G pitch, hospitality suite, clubhouse or meeting room — choose a slot on a weekly availability calendar, see an estimated cost, and send a request. You need a free account, and the club confirms by email and invoices you. Nothing is paid online. It runs on a small Supabase database alongside an otherwise static site. The point isn't automation for its own sake: it's that someone thinking about hiring the pitch on a Sunday evening can ask straight away, instead of having to remember to ring on a weekday.

2. A proper home for sponsors. A partners page, and clear pages explaining matchday sponsorship and the 200 Club, give local businesses an easy way to see how to get involved. When a sponsor asks what they get for their money, a site that looks like the club takes itself seriously is part of the answer.

3. Linking to the ticketing and shop provider you already use. Needham's tickets and merchandise are sold through a third-party store, KTCKTS. I didn't build the checkout or the payments — and I don't think a club should build its own if a good provider already does it. What the website can do is make that store easy to find: a clear shop page, links in the right places, and nothing between a supporter and buying a ticket.

4. Keeping people coming back. Fixtures, results and the league table on the homepage, the club's YouTube channel linked in, programmes and news kept up to date. None of that takes money directly. But a site people check every week is a site they'll see the shop, the hire options and the sponsors on.

The same idea applies to your business

You don't need to be a football club. The pattern is: find the thing people can only ask for when you're there to answer, and let the website take the request when you're not.

A salon could take booking requests for the quiet Tuesday slots. A trades business could take quote requests with photos while it's out on a job. A farm shop could list its Christmas hampers early. A venue could show what's free and let people ask to book. Each of these is like the Bloomfields function rooms — something you already have, that's easier to sell if people can ask for it at any time.

Why it has to feel right

A lot of the value comes from the upgrade being visible. If your website looks like it hasn't been touched since 2014, people notice, and so do potential sponsors. It's also why I aim for fast, well-structured builds: the quality has to be real, because people can tell. That's my view, not a measured result — but it's why I care about it.

Where to start

Ask one question of your own business: what do people currently have to phone, visit or wait for? That's the first thing worth putting on the website. Booking requests, quote requests, links to where they can buy — pick the one with the most friction and build the site around it.
